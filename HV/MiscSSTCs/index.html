SSTCs

A Bunch of Miscellaneous Solid-State Tesla Coils

2022-2024

Background

The Solid-State Tesla Coil, or SSTC, is a Tesla coil that uses a solid-state inverter to switch the primary coil at the secondary coil's resonant frequency. This is different from the traditional method used by the original Tesla coils where a resonant primary circuit excited by a spark gap was used. For switching the primary coil, we can use a number of different switching topologies, typically using MOSFET or IGBT transistor switches.

[2022] Ramped Half-Bridge SSTC

It is known that tesla coil sparks form different shapes if the power level is rapidly changed. If the power level starts low and is rapidly increased over a few milliseconds, the arcs produced are much longer and straighter than they would normally be. This is best achieved by precicely modulating the Tesla coil power, however a much easier way to achieve a lesser form of this effect us by running the coil off of un-smoothed rectified mains, and only running during the increasing half of the cycle. This method of running the coil is typically called "staccato".

This is achieved by using a circuit to detect the zero voltage crossing on mains, then trigger a 555 timer circuit to turn on for a short, adjustable period of time. The staccato interrupter design was taken from a similar coil by Gao Guangyan, which can be found here. The coil design was additionally inspired by a coil by LabCoatz. The Tesla coil driver used is just a standard half-bridge or full-bridge SSTC driver, which has been used by countless people at this point.

[2023] Tiny Driverless Push-Pull Class E SSTC

I wanted to try out some continuous-wave (CW) SSTCs, as well as some smaller and simpler stuff after building my fairly large DRSSTC 1. The biggest roadblock to running an SSTC in continuously is that the switching devices will rapidly overheat. The majority of this heat is produced from switching losses, both from high currents present when switching off, and high voltages present when switching on. The class-E switching topology reduces turn-off losses using a parallel capacitance to slow the voltage rise time. In some cases at very high frequencies, the output capacitance of the switching device may be enough that no additional capacitor is needed. This on its own isn't enough, as the increased capacitance will cause increased losses as it is discharged turning back on. in order to naturally pull the voltage back down, a tuned RLC circuit (where the R is typically just the load) is used. If tuning is correct, this forms a critically damped circuit that will reach zero volts with zero dV/dt the moment the switch turns back on, completely eliminating turn-on losses. The result is a highly efficient switching topology.

The circuit designed is similar to the 24V push-pull SSTC described earlier on this page, except that the ordinary push-pull inverter is replaced with a double-ended class-E, or push-pull class-E inverter. This functions the same way as an ordinary class-E circuit, but with two of them operating 180 degrees out of phase, giving twice the output voltage. I also removed all of the interrupter circuitry, as with everything tuned right this will be able to run continuously. Notice that there are some capacitors placed in parallel with the tank circuit. These serve the same purpose as the drain-source capacitors, since only one MOSFET will be on at a time. It's just easier to do it this way for tuning purposes, although there is still smaller capacitors directly connected to the MOSFETs because the lower inductance means there will be less voltage spiking on the FETs when they turn off.

For such a simple circuit, the results are quite nice. Even this tiny heatsink was able to keep the MOSFETs cool, showing the power of class-E!

[2023] 4MHz Fixed Frequency Class-E SSTC

The Class-E switching topology is very promising for very small Tesla coils as mentioned previously. However, class-E is a very fragile thing, requiring precise tuning to function efficiently, and this makes it difficult to properly tune in a self-oscillating circuit which has a variable frequency. Therefore, a fixed frequency design could achieve a high frequency more easily. I used a 4MHz crystal oscillator module to produce a stable 4MHz signal, which was then fed into a small gate driver. The gate driver chip is not able to provide the necessary power to drive a larger MOSFET at 4MHz, so instead it's used to drive a TO-220 MOSFET for a smaller class-E preamplifier, who's output will then drive a TO-247 MOSFET which switches the bigger class-E circuit supplying the primary coil.

The driver board contains an on board interrupterer (which was never actually used...), and all the driving circuitry up to and including the class-E preamplifier.

The output of the preamplifier is coupled to the larger MOSFET gate through an RF transformer and impedance matching network.

[2023] Tiny Portable Push-Pull SSTC (Revision 1)

After building a few small tesla coils, I wanted to make one that was truly portable, small enough to fit in a backpack and powered from a wall adapter. I chose a familiar push-pull design, similar to the one used in my 24V push-pull SSTC which had proven to work well with low input voltages. I modified the interrupter circuit to be more adjustable for a wide range of spark effects. I also chose different MOSFETs. After looking through various available FETs from Mouser Electronics, I chose TI's CSD19534KCS, rated for 100V and 100A with an on resistance of 16.5mΩ.

The circuit was assembled on copper clad board and perfboard for initail testing, and a small secondary coil was wound and coated. Tests showed everything working well!

With the prototype working well, I decided to make this build a little nicer by designing and ordering a custom PCB for the first time! Everything seemed to work well.

However, the coil soon started to kill MOSFETs. The circuit contains an LC filter at the secondary feedback node, which when present seems to shift the operating frequency slightly closer to resonance and increase output. For reasons unknown to me to this day, when this was enabled massive sharp voltage spikes would occur on the MOSFET gate at the start of each pulse. I discussed this issue with a number of people and we came to the conclusion that it was either related to the miller capacitance coupling over the voltage spiking on the drain when the MOSFET turns off, or perhaps the LC inductor was picking up some higher frequency noise from some part of the circuit and coupling it through the gate drive transformer. However, none of the ideas we tried were able to fix the issue, so ultimately I abandoned the LC filter portion of the circuit.

Tiny SSTC Revision 2

With the decrease in output as a result of abandoning the LC feedback filter led me to start looking at alternative circuits. There is circuit that is somewhat popular, which is similar to the push-pull circuit used but with an added resonant capacitor, and constant current input through inductors. This allows for higher primary currents as well as zero-voltage switching, achieving a higher efficiency and potentially higher output. Crude testing by adding capacitors and inductors to the existing circuit showed promising results, so I designed and ordered a new PCB. I had some leftover IRFP260MPBF MOSFETs from a separate project, which I chose to use here.

A little bit of tuning to get close to zero-voltage switching... Actually, if coupling and loading is just right, then class-E switching can be achieved for even higher efficiency. Unfortuantely this is very sensitive to the loading and the exact resonant frequency of the circuit, so it is not stable. This is the closest I was able to get consistently.

Aaand that's basically it! I assembled an appropriate wall adapter and put everything together.

[2023-2024] Portable Medium-Sized Half Bridge SSTC

After designing the tiny portable SSTC and getting used to designing and ordering my own PCBs, I wanted to try something similar but a bit bigger. I chose to use the classic, proven SSTC circuit which takes secondary coil feedback, cleans it up with a schmitt trigger, and then feeds it into gate drivers controlling a half-bridge through a gate drive transformer. I added the same on-board I designed for the tiny portable SSTC, and a D flip-flop which would prevent the gate drive being interrupted half way through a cycle. I wanted to use a higher operating frequency to allow for a somewhat smaller secondary coil and topload, so I tried something new and used fast SiC cascode FETs (UJ4C075033K3S) instead of the usual IGBTs. These are suited for much higher frequencies than similarly rated IGBTs, but are unfortunately a lot more expensive and sensitive. I chose the newer UCC27524 gate drivers because of their faster rise and fall time, and higher voltage capability.

To make everything easily portable, I wanted to make a small power supply for the driver, and an input filter and voltage doubler for the half bridge, all of which could go on their own boards mounted above the main board. Wanting to get some practice designing switching power supplies, I designed a simple ~21W flyback converter for the driver, using the popular TNY290 chip which contains its own switching MOSFET and a number of protection features. For the input interference filter and voltage doubler, I used a standard filter setup with a full-bridge rectifier that can be optionally switched to a voltage doubler by adding a jumper.

Next, a new secondary coil was wound on 2.375" PVC pipe, and coated with polyurethane as usual.

And while the new secondary coil was drying, I decided to do some tests with an old one. Things looked very promising with 170V on the bus!

Unfortunately, I ran into issues with the UCC27524 gate driver chips dying. I found that they would tend to die when the bridge was tested at higher voltages. I eventually determined that the likely cause was voltage spiking on the chip's power input as a result of high lead inductance, caused by using a through-hole part in a socket. This would have likely been fine if I was using an older chip like the UCC27321, because their slower rise time would result in a lower dI/dt and therefore smaller transient spikes. I decided to try using a somewhat slower chip that would work as a drop-in replacement, the UCC27424. This seemed to fix the problem, so I could finally try the coil with 340V on the bus, and the results were excellent!... Until...

That's 2 SiC cascode FETs failing explosively, just off camera. I initially wasn't sure what caused this, but later testing showed that when arcing to a grounded object, which had happened just before the explosion, the circuit would lock onto a much lower frequency than usual, where it would begin to resonate aggressively producing massive primary currents which would easily destroy the half bridge. At this point I had also realized that my switching frequency was only going to be about 400kHz, and a fast IGBT would easily be able to handle that. So, I switched to the much cheaper and, at the lower frequency far superior, FGA40T65SHD. I also added some common mode filters in the form of ferrite rings on the gate drive transformer to reduce noise coupled through it, in case that was causing issues. It was when testing with the new IGBTs that I realized the previously mentioned lower frequency resonating problem.

I'm still not exactly sure the cause of this problem, but at this point I had found several problems in the original board and I new that this new problem could be prevented from destroying IGBTs by simply implementing some overcurrent protection circuitry. So, I decided to go ahead and design a new and improved board. Below is the new schematic and board, notice the added overcurrent protection, and I also removed some things I found were unnecessary.

This time, I made sure not to use IC sockets for the gate drivers. I also stuck with the slower UCC27424, as they were perfectly sufficient. The new board can be seen on the left below, compared to the old one on the right.

The gate waveforms, again, look quite nice. Of course, it's important to remember that the waveforms will look somewhat different when a load is applied due to the miller capacitance. Ideally I would have taken these measurements with an inductive dummy load attached.

And, well, the new design pretty much just worked! A series capacitor was added to the primary coil to form an LC circuit, which would be driven a decent bit above resonance to slightly lower the switching current and slightly raise the primary current while having more turns on the primary. The secondary and primary coils were added to a 3D-printed mount, and an acrylic tube was added around the secondary coil for protection, and because I think it looks nice. Everything went together very well, and the output of the final device was excellent!
