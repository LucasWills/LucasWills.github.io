QCW1

Quasi-Continuous Wave Dual-Resonant Solid State Tesla Coil

2023-2024

Background

The QCW - short for "Quasi-Continuous Wave dual-resonant solid state tesla coil" - is a type of dual-resonant solid state tesla coil (DRSSTC) which operates for much longer on-times than a typical DRSSTC, typically 7-20ms as opposed to <300µs. The term 'quasi-continuous' is, I believe, borrowed from laser terminology. Many fascinating and beautiful plasma effects can be achieved by a QCW, but one of the most well-known and stunning effects is achieved by slowly ramping the power output of the coil up from a low power to a high power in a few milliseconds. The result of this is incredibly straight, 'sword-like' arcs, which can end up more than ten times the length of the actual coil if tuned properly.

This brings up a few unique design challenges, in addition to the existing difficulty in designing an ordinary DRSSTC. First, is the question of how to build a DRSSTC so it can run continuously for several milliseconds without blowing up. In a standard DRSSTC of the size we will be building, the resonant frequency will be on the order of a few hundred kilohertz, and the primary peak currents can be greater than 300A. This is fine when running TO-247 IGBTs with 200µs pulses, but there are no semiconductors currently available for an affordable price which can handle these currents at these frequencies for more than a millisecond. To get around this problem, we will have to build the primary circuit to a much higher impedance than is typical. Of course, in an ideal resonant circuit the impedance is always zero, but in the case of a tesla coil the primary circuit is damped by its coupling to the much more lossy secondary coil, as well as to the formation of plasma. This is what normally limits the peak current in the primary circuit, other than the on-time. To keep primary current below ~150A, we will use a relatively high impedance primary coil and resonant capacitor bank, something greater than 40Ω. Additionally, higher coupling between primary and secondary coil than is typical for a DRSSTC will be beneficial for this, allowing faster energy transfer from primary to secondary circuit. This causes an additional issue in raising coupling while avoiding arcing between the primary and secondary coils. Some people have attempted to raise coupling in QCW coils using a large ferrite core inside the secondary (see this coil by Dave Knierim and this coil by jmartis2) , but this presents problems with insulation to prevent internal arcing, not to mention the high cost of large ferrites. Instead, we will simply get the primary as close as possible to the secondary while maintaining enough distance to prevent arcing. By using FEMM to optimize the primary geometry, we can still get a coupling coefficient greater than 0.4 while maintaining good distance.

The second design problem I want to talk about is, how do we actually achieve a smooth ramp up in power over a few milliseconds in a device that's pulling potentially over ten kilowatts from the bus capacitors? There are a few approaches people have taken to achieve this, but two in particular have been shown to be effective. These are 1: powering the tesla coil's inverter through a fast-responding buck converter capable of rapidly modulating its output voltage, and 2: using a modulated phase-shift in one leg of the full-bridge inverter to essentially add dead time to the inverter according to an input signal.

The phase-shift full-bridge (PSFB) seems attractive at first, because it doesn't require any additional power electronics to be implemented. However, it has the downside that it's guarenteed that at least half of the semiconductors will not be switching at zero current, resulting in much greater switching losses and therefore requiring more powerful and/or faster semiconductors. Additionally, to evenly spread this load across the full-bridge requires the phase shift to alternate legs, which significantly complicates driver circuitry. While this could still be implimented using individual logic gates, it would likely be more practical to switch to an FPGA to impliment this.

The buck converter route on the other hand, requires more power electronics but is significantly easier to control, while additionally having the advantage that the inverter can be tuned as close as possible to perfect zero current and zero voltage switching. This massively reduces switching losses at the frequencies we are working at, allowing our individual TO-247 IGBTs to be ran somewhat above their continuous current rating without significant heating. For these reasons, I chose to go with the buck converter method.

Initial Design Specifications

"Good" performance for a QCW is generally considered to be arcs more than 10 times the secondary coil height. I was shooting for arcs around 50 inches, so our secondary coil will be around 5 inches tall. Based on similar projects by other people, and my own experience and calculations, I came up with the following design specifications. Some of these would end up being changed later on.

  • Based on UD2.9 Pulse Skip driver, developed by Steve Ward et al.
  • 3.5" Diameter 5" tall Secondary Coil, 700 turns of 34 AWG wire
  • 3D printed 8"x 2" toroid form wrapped in aluminum tape, Giving unloaded secondary resonance around 300kHz
  • Upper pole operation above 400kHz. Unloaded primary resonant frequency must be a bit higher than unloaded secondary to achieve upper pole operation with the simple driver
  • 10-15 turn primary coil made from 12 AWG solid wire
  • 10nF 10kV primary capacitor bank, 10 series x 3 parallel B32642B0333J PP film pulse capacitors
  • Full-bridge inverter using FGH75T65SHD IGBTs
  • Planned 150A maximum primary current
  • Bus modulated design, using a large asynchronous buck converter with 2 parallel FGH75T65SHD IGBTs and a DSEI2X31-10B diode
  • 100µH buck inductor wound on large iron powder core, 24µF capacitance integrated into the inverter as decoupling capacitors
  • Buck converter controlled by a PWM signal over fiber optic cable. The signal is generated by and arduino pro micro using a modified design by Finn Hammer

Module 2: The Buck Converter

The buck converter needs to be able to take an input of 340V, from doubled mains voltage, and be able to drop that down to the whole range from 0-340V, while being able carefully rise the output voltage acording to the PWM signal recieved from the arduino. The precise voltage given by the converter is not critical, but rather that the voltage responds quickly and is decently stable.

If it was desired to rapidly and precicely lower the inverter power as well, for example to achieve audio modulation, then a synchronous buck converter would be necessary, basically a very powerful class-D audio amplifier. However, we're only interested in precisely ramping up the voltage, and the load is roughly constant so an asynchronous buck converter works just fine.

I chose to build the buck converter heavily based on a design by Gao Guangyan's design here, which is in turn based on previous work by Steve Ward. It is a relatively simple closed-loop asynchronous buck converter driver based on an ultrafast comparator with some hysteresis, comparing an input signal and the output of the buck converter. I wanted to also implement overcurrent protection to the buck converter in case something goes wrong, so I added a latch taking input from a comparator that would compare the output of some current measuring device to an adjustable reference voltage. If tripped, the input to the isolated gate driver is forced low until the circuit is powered off or manually reset.

Of course, the buck converter needs to be able to handle hard-switching at current peaks above 100A, which means we need some pretty serious silicon. I already chose FGH75T65SHD IGBTs for the inverter, so to make things a bit simpler I decided to use these for the buck converter as well. These are rated for 150A continuous when cold, but since we're hard switching such a high current and some heating will definitely occur, I chose to put 2 in parallel. For the freewheeling diode, I had recently gotten some large and fast SOT-227 diodes for cheap and figured this was a good opportunity to use one. They are DSEI2X31-10B, 2x 30A diodes. The diodes are carrying a lower average current and are not subject to the same switching stress as the IGBTs, so the 60A continuous rating is fine. As with any switching converter, decoupling capacitors are crucial. I used 2 1µF PP film capacitors, each placed as close as possible to one of the IGBTs.

For the output LC filter on the buck converter, the inductor and capacitor need to both be able to handle the very high currents involved. The capacitor is the easy part, I chose to use two large 1.1kV 10µF PP film capacitors, which will double as decoupling on the inverter board. The inverter will also have 2 smaller 2.2µF snubber capacitors, bringing the total capacitance to about 24µF.

The inductor on the other hand, isn't so simple. Buying an appropriate inductor for this amount of current would be very pricey, so instead we make it ourselves. The hard part is that it has to be able to handle >100A without getting near saturation. Additionally, because the switching frequency of our simple buck converter will be relatively low, potentially only 10kHz when operating at minimum power, we need a cutoff frequency significantly below this. I chose to aim for 100µH, giving a cutoff frequency of 3.2kHz. Making an inductor with this inductance that can handle >100A requires the use of a very beefy core. Some people have had success using various shapes of ferrites, however the simplest approach is to use an iron powder ring. There are many different iron powder materials, but they generally have a fairly low permeability and high saturation flux density, making them a good choice for our inductor. I used a large toroid of the -2 material, which has been shown to be suitable for QCW buck converters by Gao Guangyan with very good results. The inductor was formed by painstakingly winding around 65 turns of 12 AWG solid-core wire around the large iron powder ring.

Now for the driver, as previously mentioned I had added overcurrent protection to the circuit. To actually sense the buck converter current, I chose to use a large Hall effect sensor which I had gotten from the same person who got me those diodes. The sensor, HAS 100-S, was fairly easy to implement but did need it's own positive and negative 15V rails. The final driver is shown below.

Then, all that's left for the buck converter was to put the driver together. At the time I was still doing most of my work on perfboards so...

Note the plastic case covered in aluminum tape. It's important to keep all the EM waves from the coil away from the driver circuitry.

Module 3a: The Inverter

The inverter needs to supply our primary resonant tank circuit. Using a full-bridge inverter, the switches will need to handle switching >150A at up to 450kHz. We will be tuning the inverter for zero current turn-off and zero voltage turn-on of the switches, or as close to that as possible. To minimize switching losses, we need to chose very fast IGBT switches and drive them with a very strong gate drive signal. I chose the FGH75T65SHD IGBTs from Onsemi. These are rated for 150A and 650V, and have quite fast switching times. There is a rule of thumb that the total switching time should be less than 10% of the period. Adding up the turn on delay, rise time, turn off delay and fall time gives us 178.4ns of switching time, which means these IGBTs are usable up to around 560kHz. Of course this is just a rough rule of thumb, but we can see what we should be just fine at 450kHz. The rating of 150A might seem too low, however because we are switching soft, and will only be running for <20ms up to 5 times a second, the 150A rating is more than sufficient. Of course, we will need to ensure that the IGBTs stay cool to avoid thermal runaway.

The most crucial part in designing the inverter is ensuring that there is a low inductance current path from the decoupling capacitors to the IGBTs and back. Low inductance between the high and low side IGBTs of each leg is also important. If inductance in these areas is not minimized, we will have huge voltage spikes at the switching nodes that can very easily destroy the IGBTs. Minimizing stray inductance is best done using a laminated bus layout, where the positive and negative bus are planes on opposite sides of an insulating board, resulting almost all inductance being cancelled out. A slightly less optimal but easier to construct layout is to have the positive and negative bus side by side on one side of the board, and the bridge outputs side by side the other way on the other side. This layout is showcased well in this thread and has been found to work quite well. This layout, slightly modified to fit my huge decoupling/buck output capacitors, is what I used. It was constructed using blank circuit board stock and a dremel cutter and a drill, cleaned up and soldered.

Notice that some of the holes have had the copper around them cleared out using a large countersink bit. These are the holes where smaller decoupling capacitors will attach to the positive and negative bus, however the top side of the board is the bridge output, so the large clearance is very important to minimize the likelyhood of a very unpleasant 340V short circuit.

Those huge black blocks are the previously mentioned 1.1kV 10µF PP film capacitors which serve a dual purpose as the output LC filter capacitor for the buck converter, as well as decoupling for the full-bridge inverter. For additional decoupling with slightly less impedance, smaller 2.2µF film capacitors were placed directly between the IGBTs. Maybe it's overkill but, spoilers, this project never killed a single IGBT so I think I made the right choice.

Next thing to do was to add the control connections to the bridge. I picked a suitably sized high-permeability ferrite to use for the gate drive transformer. Then, I disassembled some Cat 5 ethernet cable and wound all 8 cables into one big cable. the 4 white wires will be the primary, all in parallel, and the 4 colored wires will be the 4 secondary coils. Each IGBT gets its own secondary. We need to get the fastest possible rise and fall times on the gate drive signals to minimize switching losses in the inverter and counteract the miller effect. To do this we need to minimize leakage inductance in the gate drive transformer. All of the cables must be tightly wound together on the core and, once they leave the core, each separate coil must be tightly twisted with itself to form a twisted pair. Additionally, we should use the minimum possible number of turns without saturating the core. Some testing showed me that the core preforms fine at our working frequency with 2 turns. I used 3 turns to be safe, as saturation of the gate drive transformer would almost certainly kill the inverter.

Each IGBT gets its own gate resistor to dampen ringing caused by the small but still meaningful leakage inductance. By minimizing this inductance, a very low value can be chosen for the gate resistor which further reduces the rise time of the gate signal. It's difficult to see in the pictures, but the gate resistors are bypassed in the reverse direction by a fast diode. This will slightly increase ringing when turning off the IGBTs, but this isn't a big issue because the gate voltage will already be below -10V, the IGBTs will be driven off hard regardless of voltage ringing. Ringing when turning on is a bigger concern because of the possibility of it causing gate voltage to drop low enough for the IGBT to desaturate. The purpose of the reverse diode is to speed up IGBT turn off, which is where switching losses are the biggest concern since we will never have absolutely perfect ZCS, and additionally, the reverse diode will inject a small amount of deadtime in which both IGBTs are off, preventing the possibility of shoot through. This is not strictly necessary, shoot through is already unlikely because of the gate drive transformer topology being used, unless the IGBTs are way too slow.

Low power testing of the inverter under load shows clean waveforms coming out of the bridge. Note the ferrite bead on the primary connection to the gate drive transformer. This is simply to help prevent noise from the inverter coupling back into the driver, at the cost of some leakage inductance.

This inverter would likely be sufficient as is, and worked fine for some early tests of the complete coil, however at the suggestion of Dave Knierim on highvoltageforum.net, I decided to put some more effort into cooling the IGBTs as they were starting to get warm after runs. I cut up some 1/8" copper busbar into 1" square heat spreaders, put some ultra-high thermal conductivity insulating pads between them and the heatsink, and placed the IGBTs on top with some high performance thermal paste. Additionally, instead of using the screw hole in the IGBTs which is far from the actual silicon die, the IGBTs were held down with 3D-printed clamps I threw together in Onshape and had a friend print out of PETG (thanks Jess!). These clamps were designed to put pressure on the center of the IGBT die, giving a better thermal connection to the heat spreader.

Module 3b: The Inverter Driver

The inverter driver, or just the driver, is probably the most important part of the whole device while simultaneously being the easiest to make. And that's because dozens of talented engineers online have already designed some excellent solid state tesla coil driver circuits, which the majority of solid state tesla coils built today are heavily based upon. I chose to use the "Universal Driver 2.9", or simply UD2.9, Designed by Steve Ward, Phillip Slawinski, Bart Anderson, Eric Goodchild, Gao Guangyan, and certainly some others I am unaware of. There is a more popular UD2.7 driver, however the UD2.9 was chosen because of its pulse skipping ability, where current regulation is achieved by dropping gate drive cycles, whereas the UD2.7 simply cuts out until the next burst when the current limit is reached. Pulse skipping is desirable for QCW coils because the relatively long on-times mean that the driver cutting out when current limit is reached would significantly shorten the on-time, giving inconsistent output.

All versions of the UD2.X function as a sort of injection-locked oscillator. An initial pulse is sent to the inverter which starts the primary resonant circuit ringing, this ringing is picked up by a current transformer on the primary coil. The current from the transformer is wired back to the driver, where it is shunted by an RL circuit, which causes a slight voltage phase lead. A variable inductor is used here, so the phase lead can be tuned to cancel out the driver's propagation delay, resulting in a perfect lock to the resonant frequency and close to zero-current switching. Zero-voltage switching can be achieved with further tuning. The driver picks up the modified feedback signal with an ultrafast comparator, some simple logic is used to create the inverting and non-inverting outputs and interrupt them as necessary, and then those signals are sent to a small gate driver chip which in turn powers a small full-bridge formed by complementary mosfets. The output of the miniature full-bridge inverter is then sent to the gate drive transformer where it will go on to drive the big IGBTs.

At the time this coil was built, I had no experience with SMD soldering nor did I have the tools for it, so I chose to go with a through-hole version of the UD2.9, which was laid out by Daniel Marks and is publicly available on highvoltageforum.net. Similar to the buck converter driver, the UD2.9 was packaged in a plastic box covered in aluminum tape to protect it from the extreme EMI from the coil.

The driver needs 2 current transformers for feedback. One for feedback into the oscillator, and the other for measuring the tank current for the overcurrent detection/pulse skipping circuit. For ease of construction, cascaded current transformers were used. The first stage would be a 1:15 ratio on a fairly large ferrite toroid, and that would then pass through 2 smaller toroids, one with a 1:15 ratio again and the other with a 1:10 ratio. This gives us the equivalent of two current transformers, one with a 1:150 ratio used for oscillator feedback and the other with a 1:225 ratio used for overcurrent detection.

Module 4: The (original) Resonant Circuits

The primary coil with its resonant capacitor bank, and the secondary coil with its parasitic capacitance and topload form 2 coupled resonant circuits. This setup forms 2 resonant poles. The tesla coil could operate just fine at either one of these frequencies, however in order to get the beautiful straight sword arcs that QCW coils are known for, the operating frequency must be at least around 300kHz, ideally closer to 400kHz. A relatively low primary circuit capacitiance is desired to keep primary currents down, and a relatively large secondary circuit capacitance is desired to lessen the effects of the large electric arcs, which add a not insignificant amount of capacitance. Using a large secondary capacitance will cause this to have less of an effect. The large capacitance will also keep the voltage a bit lower, which will allow us to have a higher coupling between the primary and secondary without arcing between them. With all of this in mind, the secondary coil was chosen to be around 700 turns of 34 AWG wire on 3.5" OD PVC pipe. The topload would be a 2x8" toroid made by a 3D-printed form coated in aluminum tape. Simulations show this would give the secondary coil an unloaded resonant frequency of around 308kHz, and a loaded resonant frequency around 250kHz with a 50" arc. The primary coil would be tunable by tapping the windings at different locations, and would be roughly 22µH. The primary resonant capacitor bank would be formed by an array of smaller film capacitors. For these capacitors, I chose B32642B0333J 33nF 1kV caps from TDK due to their high current pulse capability, convenient capacitance and voltage ratings, and cost. The capacitor bank would be 3 parallel strings of 10 in series, forming a 9.9nF 10kV bank.

With the parameters decided, I got to work constructing everything. First, the 3D-printed toroid. It came out very nice, but would later be upgraded. We'll get to that in a bit.

Next, the secondary coil. The winding process is pretty straightforward, just takes a good bit of patience. After winding, the coil was coated with 3 layers of paint-on oil based polyurethane. A simple jig made from old VEX robotics parts I had from highschool was used to rotate the coil while the coating dried.

I think I made the layers a bit too thick, because the result ended up a bit wrinkly. It doesn't really matter though, it's just a bit ugly.

Now, the capacitor bank. Not much to explain here, some copper strip was used for the ends and the bank was made into 2 smaller banks in series so it can be a bit more compact. It's important to leave gaps between the strips so that the capacitors on the inside are able to get airflow to stay cool. Otherwise, the capacitors will heat unevenly and the ones on the inside could overheat and fail.

For initial testing, a crude primary coil was constructed from some random 16 AWG wire and some plastic sheet. This was rough and not at all suitable for the finished device, but it was good enough for the first few testing sessions which we'll go over later.

Eventually though, a nicer and more permanent primary would need to be built. For best QCW performance, a high coupling coefficient is desirable. This ends up being a bit tricky because of the high voltages present on the secondary coil, we can't just place them as close together as possible. Instead, we can use some more complicated coil geometry to get a high coupling while avoiding the electric fields getting too sharp. I messed with some coil geometires in FEMM, a program that can solve 2D electromagnetics problems. This gave me a nice curved coil geometry with a primary inductance of 22.5µH and a coupling coefficient of 0.44.

To construct this coil, I modeled some forms in onshape, and again had a friend print them from PETG. The forms were made to fit 12 AWG THHN wire. This was actually really annoying to wind, but with patience and a bunch of zip ties, it was evenually complete. I used a razor to cut back the insulation at a few spots on the outermost windings, to serve as tap points so we can tune the primary circuit. We want the primary circuit to be tuned as low as possible while still being tuned higher than the unloaded secondary, because this will cause the oscillator to lock to the upper pole instead of the lower pole. If it locks to the lower pole frequency, our frequency will be way too low to get straight arcs.

The original completed setup is shown below, along with a quick simulation, telling us we will have an unloaded upper pole at 440kHz, and with a 50" arc that will drop down to 410kHz. These are pretty good numbers.

The First Tests

With all the individual components ready, I could finally wire everything together and test it! The inverter and its driver were first tested at low power with feedback injected from a function generator. This showed that the driver, gate drive transformer and inverter were properly assembled and worked fine. Then, the buck converter was tested with a large dummy load. The waveform wasn't as clean as I would have liked, but this could be attributed to the fact that the dummy load was pulling much less power than the inverter will be. Even if this wasn't the case, the voltage ripple will probably not affect performance. Some of the ripple was also definitely an artifact from EMI, since nothing was shielded.

This also showed that the control box was functioning properly.

With all of the modules confirmed to work properly, everything could be roughly assembled on the garage floor and tested all together! To help avoid a catastrophic faliure, the big voltage doubler powering the buck converter was plugged in through a large lightbulb to act as a ballast and limit the current that could be pulled from the wall. So, the voltage doubler was allowed to slowly charge through the lightbulb aaanndddd...

First light!

Some 30" arcs with a 200V 8ms ramp and no tuning, pretty promising results! However, I started to run into a problem when trying to push the power any higher. My big voltage doubler couldn't store enough energy! The bus voltage was dropping so low during the burst that I couldn't push the voltage or pulse width any higher.

My bus capacitors were rated for 450V each, and I had 3 of them, which means by assembling them in parallel I could get much more energy out at 340V than with 2 in series. To do this, I had to assemble a smaller voltage doubler to power the separate parallel capacitor bank. I wasn't sure if this would really work, but some LTSpice simulation confirmed that it should be fine. An unexpected advantage of this setup is that the smaller capacitors in the voltage doubler limit the peak current that can be pulled from the wall, so we get gentler current spikes when charging up the big capacitors.

So the next day, I put all that together again and the results were spectacular!

Next up, I assembled the nicer primary, and after some tuning of the primary coil and phase lead, I got sparks up to 45 inches!

And some less well behaved arcs reminding me I should really put all this circuitry into a box...

At this point, I figured before pushing the power any higher I should probably get the zero-current and zero-voltage switching tuned as good as possible. This is done by adjusting the variable inductor in the UD2.9. Initial tuning is done with a dummy load in the form of a steel pot in place of the secondary coil. This makes it much easier since you don't have to deal with all the EMI from the secondary coil interfering with scope readings. From there, we just adjust phase lead until the voltage spiking is minimal and the turn-on time matches with the natural swing of the bridge output voltage.

Putting Everything Together

After running everything on the floor for a while, it was finally time to put things together into a big enclosure. I ordered 2 12"x18" 1/8" thick polycarbonate sheets, some aluminum angle and extrusion and made a sort of box. Then, I figured out how I wanted to fit everything inside.

Next, I of course needed power supply for everything! I cut out some slots in the aluminum angle for a plug, port and fuse. I added a big terminal block for some of the more beefy connections, and soldered up a standard interference filter to slot into it. I also needed 3 isolated low-voltage power supplies for all the control circuitry. I took the switching power supply from an old 20V laptop charger for the inverter driver, and I put together 2 isolated linear supplies for the buck converter controller. One gives +15V for the isolated gate driver, and the other gives +15V and -15V for the for the controller. 3D-printed mounts were designed and printed to hold everything in place.

Next, the bottom layer was assembled, including the buck converter's switching components and hall effect sensor, the resonant capacitor bank mount, and fan mounts.

Then, everything else was added on top!

This was also the point when I upgraded the IGBT cooling, and now with that and an actual fan blowing on the heatsinks, I was ready to crank up the power even more! The results were, again, spectacular.

Secondary Circuit Upgrades

At this point, I started to hit a wall in terms of arc size just under 60 inches. While this was already longer than my goal of 10x the secondary length, I was still hungry for more. I tried pushing current higher, and further tuning, but eventually realized what was likely happening was that the arc loading was getting too great and preventing efficient power transfer at high frequencies. To fix this, a higher secondary circuit capacitance would be required. However, doing this on its own would pull the operating frequency too low to get straight spark growth, so I would also have to decrease secondary coil inductance.

To this end, I wound a new secondary coil the same size but out of 32 AWG wire. It had a bit over 600 turns, giving an inductance around 16mH. I also used green magnet wire instead of the usual red, inspire by Gao Guangyan's QCW because I thought it looked nice. I learned my lesson with making the polyurethane layers too thick, and the new coil came out much better.

After setting up the new secondary, inital testing with a doubled up topload was very promising.

The next step to the upgrade was to make a proper new topload. Calculations told me that a 10"x3" toroid would give a capacitance around 14pF, giving our upgraded circuit an unloaded resonant frequency around 330kHz and an upper pole around 450kHz unloaded. A 60" arc will pull that down to 410kHz, both good numbers, although a little high. To get the frequency slightly lower and add additional resistance to the capacitive effect of the arcs, I wanted to try adding a small capacitor bank in parallel to the secondary coil. This is very unconventional for a tesla coil, however it has been shown to be possible before by Steve Ward. I took a somewhat different approach, instead of using bulky film capacitors I decided to try out some small but powerful C0G class 1 capacitors. I used 27 series 3kV 82pF flexible-termination capacitors, soldered into a big line and potted in 2-part epoxy resin. This gives an extra 3pF, giving an unloaded resonant frequency at 300kHz, upper pole at 430kHz, and upper pole with a 60" arc at 405kHz. It could still be a bit lower, but this seemed like it would be good enough.

So, I had the new topload printed, put assembled the capacitor bank, and put everything back together!

After further tuning the primary and phase lead, I settled on inverter max set to 180A, usually peaking around 160A, and pushed the on-time all the way up to 20ms, And... The results speak for themselves. That's a 67" arc!

However, I started having problems with the overcurrent protection on the buck converter tripping, and constantly hitting the 180A overcurrent protection on the inverter with very loud snapping noises. After recording some videos, I found the source of the problem.

That's an arc flashing over at the terminals of the resonant capacitor bank! That shows that we're reaching really impressive peak voltages in the resonant circuit, but more importantly, that flashover is not good! Fortunately, it was an easy fix. I moved the ends of the capacitor bank slightly further apart, and covered all the metal bits on the inner edge in several layers of kapton tape.

Final Results

With the capacitor bank issue fixed, primary current consistently peaks around 160A and the buck converter overcurrent is no longer tripped. With some final tuning, I got my biggest arcs yet, topping out at just over 70 inches (178 cm). That's taller than me! And an impressive 13 times the length of the secondary coil.
