DRSSTC 1

Dual-Resonant Solid State Tesla Coil

2022-2023

NOTE - This is a very old project, and was not built very well as a result of my lack of experience at the time. Were I to do this today [2026], I would do a lot of things very differently. That being said, I learned a lot from this project and the results are still pretty impressive! If you are building a similar project, read this to learn from my faliures and successes!

Background

This was my first serious build of a dual-resonant solid state tesla coil, or DRSSTC for short. The traditional Tesla coil circuit used a high-voltage resonant primary coil circuit excited by a spark gap to, in turn, excite the secondary circuit which was tuned to the same resonant requency as the primary. With solid state tesla coils however, it's much easier to have the primary coil not be a resonant circuit at all, and just use an inverter to power it at the resonant frequency of the secondary coil. However, there are two problems with this approach. The first problem is that the primary current is heavily limited by the primary coil's inductance, which limits how much total power can be achieved. You could counteract this by using less turns on the primary coil, however this will result in poorer coupling to the secondary coil, and thus worse power transfer, and also when the number of turns gets very low it becomes difficult to fine-tune things. The second problem is that, when switching a purely inductive load like this simple primary coil, the current is at a maximum at the moment the switches turn off. This results in massive switching losses and voltage spikes at the switching node which stress the switching devices, and can ultimately destroy them.

This is where the DRSSTC comes in. By adding a series capacitor to the primary coil, and matching its capacitive reactance to the inductive reactance of the primary, we can completely cancel out the impedance of the primary, leaving just the negligible resistance. This allows the primary current to ring up very high, and thus transfer much more power to the secondary coil, resulting in huge arcs.

Revision 1

Below is the schematic for the first version of the DRSSTC driver, which is essentially a standard basic SSTC driver with added overcurrent protection. It turns out, I made a mistake in this version and the complementary MOSFET output stage doesn't work properly. For this reason, the output stage was removed and replaced with 2 gate driver chips. The unfortunate consequence of this is that the gate driver chips were only rated for 16V, so I would need to use a 1:1.5 ratio gate drive transformer to get a safe voltage on the IGBT gates.

For the inverter, I built a half-bridge inverter with the popular FGA60N65SMD IGBTs. They are rated for 180A pulsed when cool, and have been shown to handle up to 300A when switching soft in DRSSTC settings where they are only running for <300μs. I used a simple laminated bus layout to minimize stray inductance, a voltage doubler to get a 340V bus, and some beefy TVS diodes to help protect from voltage spikes. Because it's a half bridge, we will get 170V on the bridge output.

The secondary coil was made from 2.375" OD PVC pipe, with a 9.5" winding of 36 AWG wire. It was then coated in several layers of polyurethane varnish and allowed to dry. A crude topload was constructed from cardboard, duct tape and aluminum tape, and the primary coil was wound out of 14 AWG THHN wire. For the primary resonant capacitor, 2 series 940C20P15K PP film pulse capacitors were used, giving 75nF with a rating of 4kV. The secondary circuit resonant frequency was calculated to be approximately 270kHz.

A simple interrupter circuit using 555 timer chips was assembled into a steel electrical box for shielding, using coax cable to connect to the driver. Then, some current transformers were wound on small ferrite rings, and the driver was ready to test!

Once everything was determined to be working properly, I put everything together and it was time for first light.

The first tests were promising, however I quickly ran into a problem, where the interrupter was sending out much longer pulses than it should, longer than one millisecond when its supposed to be <300μs! This created some seriously impressive arcs, but I knew it was only a matter of time before this killed something... But, I wasn't sure what was causing it so I kinda just assumed it was some weird result of running everything sloppily laid out on the floor.

Nevertheless, I continued testing, slowly pushing power up with some pretty nice results...

Figuring things were working pretty well, I started working on putting things together into an enclosure. I started with making a nicer, more tunable primary coil. I chose a flat pancake style primary coil because they give a better electric field shape for preventing arcing between the primary and secondary coil, while also being easier to tap for tuning. The primary coil was constructed using wood blocks, 14 AWG solid wire for the coil and a 12 AWG single grounded winding around that to catch any arcs that jump from the topload down to the primary.

The new primary worked quite well, so I moved on to putting all of the circuitry together more nicely. I assembled an EMI filter for the input, used a little switching power supply pulled out of something (I don't remember what) to power the driver board, and put some shielding over the driver to protect it from the extreme EMI from the coils.

Then, I screwed everything together and got ready for more testing. Remember that issue I mentioned with the interrupter sending pulses way too long? Well, that started happening again, and after a particularly long pulse, one of the IGBTs died, and took out the other IGBT, bridge rectifier and input filter along with it. I had forgotten to add a proper fuse...

So that was a bit of a wake up call. I eventually determined the cause of the issue to be a faulty potentiometer in the interrupter, perhaps being made worse by interference getting picked up by the interrupter cable and messing with the 555 timer circuits. So I replaced the IGBTs, rectifier and input filter, and built a new interrupter and reciever for the driver using high quality potentiometers and fiber optic cable to prevent interference being picked up. I also moved the top of the enclosure further up to make the magnetic field from the primary coil a bit weaker by the point it reaches the control circuitry.

Oh, and I added an actual fast-blow fuse.

All of these changes seemed to completely fix the issues, and the next tests worked went very well. At this point I was getting arcs up to 18 inches long with overcurrent cutoff set to 290A.

These are pretty decent results, arcs nearly twice the length of the secondary coil, but I wasn't quite satisfied... After all, with the new enclosure it couldn't even make arcs long enough to hit the floor! I was already brushing up against the maximum current these IGBTs have been sucessfully ran at in other people's DRSSTCs, so pushing the current any higher was pretty likely to blow them up again. I decided to get help from some people much more knowledgeable than me on highvoltageforum.net. I'd like to thank Dave Knierim in particular for giving me assistance in a number of my projects, of which this was the first one.

After a short discussion I decided the following things. First, I would upgrade my half-bridge of 2 single FGA60N65SMD IGBTs to a half bridge of 2x 2 parallel FGH75T65SHD IGBTs. This was chosen over instead upgrading to a full bridge because it will keep the output voltage the same, meaning I wouldnt have to rebuild the primary circuit to a higher impedance. The second change would be to upgrade my gate drive circuitry to instead use a 1:1 gate drive transformer with a voltage of >18V. A 1:1 gate drive transformer can be constructed with a lower leakage inductance, which will provide a faster rise and fall time for the gate drive signal, especially important since we will be driving twice the gate capacitance with two IGBTs in parallel. The only problem with this change is that my gate drive chips are, as mentioned before, only rated for 16V. I first tried to fix this by switching to higher voltage gate drivers, however the only suitable chips I could find for a reasonable price were tiny SMD chips which I had very little experience with at the time, and had some other problems when trying to implement them. Ultimately I decided to switch back to the originally intended complementary MOSFET gate drive stange, however this would require a significant change in the driver logic to work properly. My original driver had already been messed with quite a bit and I was starting to get tired of adding and removing things from it so I decided to start fresh with a tested and well known driver, that being Steve Ward's UD1.3B.

At this point I was going to by making a lot of changes, so I've decided to call this revision 2.

Revision 2

The UD1.3B is already pretty similar to the driver I had made previously, but it features an actually working MOSFET output stage. I also added undervoltage lockout and swapped the fiber reciever for one that I already had. Then I spent a day laying out the circuit to construct it on perfboard and created what is definitely the most complicated thing I've ever seen made on a single piece of perfboard. This took 2 days to solder and came out awesome but I can not emphasize enough that you should absolutely just order a PCB instead of doing this.

Then, I constructed the new double half-bridge bridge and supporting components after designing a nice low inductance layout.

The circuit board was constructed using my favorite blank copper-clad board and dremel cutter method...

Then, the new 1:1 gate drive transformer for the double bridge. Each IGBT gets its own gate resistor for hopefully more evenly matched gate signals. I used 2 pretty big ferrite rings for the gate drive transformer, which in hindsight was extremely overkill. A new set of current transformers was also assembled, using a cascaded design which both has better electrical properties and is easier to construct.

Next, I tested the new driver and half bridge at low voltage using a piece of steel over the primary as a dummy load. Everything seemed to work well, so all that was left was to put everything together again! I added a better power supply and a soft start relay and everything was ready to go.

And after cranking the overcurrent limit up to 350A and some final tuning, the results are simply spectacular. The longest measured arc length was 22 inches.

A few months later, I made a slight upgrade to the resonant capacitor bank, bringing it up from 75nF to 100nF. This allowed me to push slightly more power. These are the final results, arcs just over 22".
